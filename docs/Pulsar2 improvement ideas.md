# Building the Nexto yardstick before new machinery

**Build the Nexto match-play evaluation harness before any new training machinery,** because two of the four known problems (inflated rating, no reliable Nexto evaluation) cannot even be diagnosed without it, and every other idea below needs it to be judged. After that, cut the inference-bound collection time with measurement-gated fixes, and test at most two reset-state levers for the slow wavedash and flip mechanics, one at a time.

Done means the harness reproduces a 50 percent plus or minus 3 point Nexto-against-Nexto mirror over 800 games, and every idea you then act on has a pass-or-fail result logged against the criterion written before the run.

### Notes on the table

Rows 1 and 2 come first because both the evaluation notes and the Rocket League prior-art notes argue the harness has to exist before any training change can be judged, and the project doctrine is measurement before machinery. The only documented Rocket League protocol is Lucy-SKG's 300 single-goal matches per comparison, which at a 50 percent win rate gives about plus or minus 5.7 points at 95 percent (my own arithmetic, not in the notes); by the evaluation researcher's normal-approximation arithmetic (80 percent power, two-sided alpha 0.05), 200 games detect a 10 point shift and 800 detect 5 points. That arithmetic assumes independent games, and the variance saving from pairing in Rocket League is unmeasured, which is why row 1 measures it.

Rows 3 through 6 are ordered by what the timers allow. The inference researcher's arithmetic puts 207,872 steps at 512 agents per forward at about 406 forwards and about 11 ms each, far above any compute floor for a network of this size, so collection looks launch-bound or contention-bound rather than FLOP-bound. That is an inference from timers, not a profile, and the timer also wraps device-to-host copies and the Nexto call. If it holds, graphing and more games per forward pay and narrower layers do not. Because collection and learning are pipelined on one GPU, my reading of the timers is that once collection drops below the 4.44 s learn time, learn becomes the bound, which is why row 6 follows row 3.

Rows 7 and 8 target the convicted blocker for mechanics, which the project's own MECHANICS.md describes as sample economics: a per-instance signal-to-noise ratio of about 0.18 with roughly 80 fragments per iteration, and a rule that added density below break-even anti-teaches while density above break-even cures it. Wavedash at 11.2 percent is above break-even, so density and difficulty control are the right levers. Row 9 is cheap insurance: if the oracle fails, rows 7, 8 and 11 are wasted effort.

Row 10 is ranked below the reset levers because Pulsar2's own diagnosis does not list ball perception as a blocker. Previous-action input, proposed by one researcher, is dropped because the merge notes found both observation builders already include it.

Several cheap items did not make the table because no listed problem points to them: a GAE lambda sweep and a Muon scale audit (half a day each, [What Matters in On-Policy RL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.05990)), a HL-Gauss-style categorical critic head (the authors' own result says the benefit vanishes under deterministic dynamics, [Stop Regressing](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.03950)), extra value-only epochs ([Phasic Policy Gradient](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.04416)), and regenerative L2 or soft shrink-and-perturb on the critic trunk ([plasticity study](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19153v2)). Each should be triggered only by its own telemetry. Auxiliary prediction heads (Lucy-SKG's reward-prediction ablation, or a self-predictive head) overlap with the goal-outcome critic and reachability self-model that Pulsar2 already has, so they are held back until a linear probe shows the trunk lacks the information.

## 2. Which idea answers which known problem

Problem 1, inference-bound collection, is addressed by rows 3, 4, 5 and 6, with row 15 as a hygiene check. The order matters: row 3 decides whether row 5 is worth building, and row 4 matters on every iteration where Nexto is served, because the documented serve cost is about 3x iteration time and one older measurement discarded about 36.6 percent of simulated rows on served iterations.

Problem 2, slow short-precision mechanics, is addressed by rows 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 13. Rows 7 and 8 act on density and difficulty, row 9 tests whether the 15 Hz, tick-skip-8 interface is itself the ceiling, and rows 11 and 13 are conditional follow-ups.

Problem 3, rating inflated about 6x, is addressed by rows 2 and 12. Row 1 supplies the games that feed them.

Problem 4, no reliable evaluation against Nexto, is addressed by rows 1, 4, 12 and 14. Row 4 also raises how many Nexto games the harness can afford.

The one-lever-at-a-time merge order is as follows. First, run Pulsar2 unchanged on the owner's machine for at least two seeds, archiving spaced checkpoints and logging Nexto goal share, reference share and per-step-invariant mechanic fractions to size the noise floor, which the project has never measured. Second, build measurement tooling: rows 1 and 2, a fixed offline loader or in-trainer mechanic counters, and optionally the V3 reward-verification approach and a fixed-shot evaluation battery. Third, change one observation lever, the closed-form landing features, and only then the full prediction block if the first arm is convicted. Fourth, if a mechanic is above break-even and has plateaued, add the state-archive setter. Fifth, port individual rewards only after the dribble-density prerequisite is measured. Sixth, build the evaluation-only exploiter once Nexto goal share plateaus. The curriculum, game modes, V3 tick settings, small network, search code, V3 anchors and match context stay out.

## 4. Ideas rejected and why

A Perceiver or transformer observation encoder was rejected because Lucy-SKG's own numbers show a reduced observation (8 objects, no boost pads, 5 stacked previous actions) ran at about 4,000 against 2,000 game frames per second in 2v2 (self-reported, possibly counted per agent) and no ablation shows attention beating a padded multilayer network for a scene of at most six cars and a ball ([Lucy-SKG](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.15801)). Recurrent or gated-transformer memory cores were rejected because gains appear only on memory tasks and parameter counts balloon ([GTrXL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.06764)). Distilling a small student actor for rollouts was rejected because the importance ratio would compare two different policies ([Decoupled PPO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.00641)); training a narrower actor directly stays an open, conditional option, discussed under disagreements. Dreamer, TD-MPC2 and in-loop MuZero-style search were rejected because model-based methods win per sample but lose per wall-clock in fast simulators (Dream to Fly took about 240 hours, [Dream to Fly](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.14377)) and search multiplies inference calls.

Replacing the potential-based zero-sum rewards with Lucy-SKG's multiplicative reward combination was rejected because it rests on one self-reported paper and cannot be attributed without the harness. Large-scale behavior cloning from replays was rejected for now: the only top-bot precedent gives qualitative evidence only, replays lack aerial inputs, and the cloned agent "looks competent but lacks consistency and precision" ([replay-pretraining](https://github.com/Rolv-Arild/replay-pretraining)). Skill discovery (METRA and relatives), hindsight relabeling proper, learned action repetition (FiGAR, TempoRL), a 30 Hz control rate, and learned or preference-based rewards were rejected for lack of evidence on precision timing, redundancy with existing heads, or worsening problem 1 ([METRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08887), [FiGAR](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.06054), [TempoRL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.05262)).

A full AlphaStar league, Pipeline PSRO, population-based training, and a rebuilt MAP-Elites archive were rejected: TStarBot-X needed 144 V100 GPUs for 57 days, and the MAP-Elites failure matches the known cause of fitness measured against a moving opponent ([TStarBot-X](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13729), [GAME](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.06617), [Pipeline PSRO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08555v1)). Int8 and FP8 inference, multi-process inference, and action caching for Nexto were rejected because the model is launch-bound, the 3090 has no FP8, and continuous states do not repeat. AOT-compiled and TensorRT actors were deprioritized behind plain graphs because a network of about seven matrix-multiply layers has little to fuse ([AOTInductor docs](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/main/user_guide/torch_compiler/torch.compiler_aot_inductor.html), [TensorRT docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/tensorrt/latest/inference-library/capabilities.html)). Distilling Nexto into a student for sparring (4 to 6 days) was deprioritized because it caps the ceiling and a weaker student is not a proxy; real Nexto must remain the yardstick. COMA and QMIX-style credit assignment were rejected for a 1v1 goal.

## 5. Unresolved questions and unverified claims

Whether any bot beats Nexto in 1v1 is unknown from the opened sources. The best Nexto result is Lucy-SKG's 300 to 4 in 2v2 single-goal matches after 1B steps, self-reported, with the paper's text and table disagreeing on when wins begin (200M against 300M steps). The press figure of "250,000 hours" of self-play for Nexto is secondhand, its unit is not stated, and it should not be used as a budget. The prior-art researcher's arithmetic that 1B steps is about 7 hours and 10B steps about 3 days at 38.6k steps/s is exact arithmetic, but it says only that the goal is not ruled out by raw step count; the idea that Pulsar2's 39 percent goal share after 3.3B steps points to a recipe or measurement problem rather than a step shortage is a hypothesis.

Open measurements: whether collection is launch-bound or contention-bound (every speedup figure in rows 3 through 6 is an estimate, and no benchmark of a graphed small network on a 3090 under WSL2 was found); how many games the owner actually runs (the inference notes say 256, the merge notes describe 1024); how much of the 4.44 s learn time is the Muon optimizer; the noise floor for Pulsar2's goal counters (never measured); the variance saving from paired games; the current-lineage wavedash rate and Nexto goal-share slope (the clone holds older lineages); and whether Pulsar2 already has weight averaging or value normalization. Facts about Nexto that were not found: its version and skill setting in Lucy-SKG's test, its tick skip and observation contents from primary documents, and any community benchmarking protocol.

Unverified claims: the 6x inflation mechanism rests on the owner's analysis and general rating principles; the LEAGUE_RECON figures come from an older league-era run; the Lucy-SKG attribution of Necto's architecture and tick skip is secondhand; the 3090 bandwidth and tensor-throughput specifications were quoted from memory; and Discord-lore claims about Nexto's training schedule were not verified and are not used. On the RLGymBotV3 side, the 103-float block's horizon and layout, the RocketSim version, RLBot's ball-prediction source, whether V3's exploiters or curriculum improved V3 at all, and whether StateBank records self-play or human play are all unknown. The offline match-play tools in the Pulsar2 repository cannot load current checkpoints, per its own inventory document.

## 6. Evidence quality and disagreements between researchers

Almost nothing here is independently replicated in Rocket League. The measured evidence is of three kinds: Pulsar2's own measurements (wavedash at 11.2 percent with a signal-to-noise ratio of about 0.18, takeoff at 0 of 300 until reset seeding, the League recon numbers, the landing-probe results, the 294 against 48.9 Elo gap), vendor documentation and tutorial benchmarks for graphs and mixed precision, and the system-scale results of OpenAI Five, Sample Factory and TStarBot-X. Everything else is claimed or cross-domain.

For each row: rows 1 and 2 rest on chess-engine practice and one badminton paper (cross-domain), plus the measured Pulsar2 counters. Rows 3, 5 and 6 rest on vendor benchmarks of other models; the Pulsar2 timers are the only measured part. Row 4 has no external source. Rows 7 and 8 combine Pulsar2's measured seeding result with robotics, grid-world and Atari papers (cross-domain, [Go-Explore](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41586-020-03157-9) is the strongest). Row 9 is a design, not a cited method. Row 10 combines the measured probe with one robotics paper. Row 11 has a single hobbyist anecdote ([Habr](https://habr.com/en/articles/773454)) and Lucy-SKG's 0.7 replay-state sampling, with no ablation. Rows 12 to 15 each rest on one or two sources from other domains.

Several researchers read only abstracts or fetched pages without bodies. The exploration researcher opened only arXiv abstract pages for every external paper, including Go-Explore, PLR, Backplay and DeepMimic. The merge researcher read MuZero, EfficientZero, SimBa, BRO, the plasticity paper, Andrychowicz et al. and AlphaStar at abstract or partial level. The value-learning researcher saw only abstracts for SimbaV2, SPO and ReDo. The prior-art researcher saw only the abstract of the SMU Rocket Learn paper and could not retrieve the Ripple report body, the Nexto repository page or primary Nexto documents. The evaluation researcher saw the first few thousand characters of the League recon, and the multi-agent researcher did not read the Pulsar2 repository at all. Researchers worked with budgets of roughly 13 to 22 tool calls. Several sources are very recent preprints with no replication (for example [ShuttleArena](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.25246), [Generals.io](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.23348), [HL-Gauss for PPO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.02181), [SingularClip](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18319)).

The researchers disagree in five places. First, replay-derived reset states: the prior-art researcher lists them as a candidate with a 2 to 4 day effort and points to Lucy-SKG's 0.7 replay-state sampling; the imitation researcher warns that replays are lossy (24 to 27 Hz, no aerial inputs, no jump or flip flags) and prefers the bot's own-play archive first; the merge researcher treats the V3 StateBank as the nearer equivalent but a rule violation. The table follows the imitation view, placing the own-play archive (row 8) ahead of replays (row 11). Second, what limits inference: the inference researcher suggests launch-bound collection that depends little on width, while the multi-agent researcher estimates 1.5 to 2x from a narrower actor, and the merge researcher says width is not the lever. Row 3 settles this by measurement, and the narrower-actor idea waits on it. Third, the order of work: the evaluation and prior-art researchers both put the harness first, which the table follows; the exploration researcher ranks adaptive resets first and does not mention the harness. Fourth, the interface ceiling: the local MECHANICS.md calls the hypothesis dead because Nexto wavedashes at tick skip 8, while the imitation researcher treats an oracle test as settling an open question; row 9 is cheap enough to resolve it. Fifth, auxiliary heads: the prior-art researcher proposes Lucy-SKG's reward-prediction head as a candidate while the multi-agent and imitation researchers judge the overlap with existing critics too large, so it stays probe-gated.

## Conclusion

The notes point to a single bottleneck beneath the four problems, which is that the project cannot currently tell a good change from a bad one: the offline match-play tools cannot load current checkpoints, the rating is inflated, and the Nexto counters have too little power. Fixing that is cheaper than any training idea in the list and may by itself show whether the "days on one 3090" goal is a step-count problem or a recipe problem. The most useful single new fact would come from the row 3 timers, because they decide whether the ranked compute work is graphs, batch size, or nothing at all.

For the RLGymBotV3 merge, the finding is mostly restraint: of twelve pieces, none is a drop-in, four are worth porting later behind a measurement (ball prediction, StateBank, individual rewards, an evaluation-only exploiter), and the best asset may be its verification harnesses rather than its learning machinery.
